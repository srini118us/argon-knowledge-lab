# Postmortem PM-2025-014: Carrier label outage

- **Document ID:** PM-2025-014
- **Version:** v1
- **Title:** Carrier label outage
- **Date:** 2025-08-06
- **Status:** Final
- **Incident date:** 2025-08-04
- **Service affected:** carrier-gateway

This is a blameless postmortem. The goal is to understand how the system and our processes allowed this to happen, not to assign fault to individuals.

## Summary

On 2025-08-04, carrier-gateway was unable to obtain shipping labels from SwiftShip for an extended period. Orders continued to be accepted by Orion, but shipments could not be booked and warehouses could not print labels, so outbound parcels piled up until the problem was resolved.

**Root cause:** a retry storm from carrier-gateway exceeded the SwiftShip rate limit, which was then 50 requests per second. Once SwiftShip began rejecting requests for exceeding the limit, carrier-gateway treated those rejections as retryable failures and retried them, which generated even more traffic, which caused even more rejections. The system held itself in that state until traffic was manually reduced.

## Impact

- carrier-gateway could not book shipments or retrieve labels for the duration of the incident.
- Warehouses were unable to dispatch parcels that needed new labels. Work that had already been labelled before the incident was not affected.
- Customers did not see errors when placing orders, but deliveries were delayed. Support received contacts from customers asking why their order had not shipped.
- No data was lost. All affected shipment requests were eventually completed after the incident, some through resubmission and a smaller number by hand.

## Timeline

Times are approximate and reconstructed from alerts, logs and the incident channel.

- **Morning, normal operation.** Traffic to carrier-gateway was at its usual level for the day of week.
- **Trigger.** A brief slowdown on the SwiftShip side caused a batch of carrier-gateway requests to time out. carrier-gateway began retrying them according to its configured policy.
- **Amplification.** Retries were added on top of normal new traffic. Combined request volume to SwiftShip crossed the 50 requests per second rate limit. SwiftShip started rejecting requests with rate limit responses.
- **Self sustaining state.** carrier-gateway classified rate limit responses as transient failures and retried them too. Each original request could now produce several outbound attempts. Even after SwiftShip's original slowdown ended, our own retry traffic kept us above the limit.
- **Detection.** carrier-gateway error rate alerts fired and paged the on-call engineer. Shortly afterwards, warehouse leads reported in the support channel that labels were not printing.
- **Initial diagnosis.** The on-call engineer checked the SwiftShip status page, which showed no ongoing incident. This initially pointed the investigation towards a problem on our side, and time was spent checking recent deploys and credentials, neither of which turned out to be relevant.
- **Key insight.** The on-call engineer noticed that the bulk of failures were rate limit rejections rather than timeouts, and that the retry count graph was climbing steeply. The incident was escalated following the escalation path in RB-CARRIER-01.
- **Mitigation.** Retries were temporarily disabled in carrier-gateway configuration. Outbound traffic dropped below the rate limit within minutes, and new requests began succeeding.
- **Recovery.** Requests that had failed during the incident were resubmitted in controlled batches, with the error rate checked between batches. Retries were then re enabled.
- **Resolution.** Label generation returned to normal and the backlog was cleared.

## Root cause analysis

### What happened

The trigger, a short slowdown on the carrier side, was ordinary. Third party APIs have bad minutes, and carrier-gateway is expected to ride through them. The outage happened because our retry behaviour turned a short external blip into a long internal one.

Three factors combined:

1. **Retry budget per request was generous.** RB-CARRIER-01 set the maximum retries per carrier request at a level that was reasonable for an isolated failure but multiplied total traffic heavily when many requests failed at once.
2. **Rate limit rejections were treated as retryable.** carrier-gateway did not distinguish "the carrier is temporarily broken" from "you are sending too much". Retrying a rate limit rejection immediately is the one thing guaranteed to make it worse.
3. **No cap on total outbound traffic.** Nothing in carrier-gateway limited the aggregate rate of requests sent to SwiftShip. Each request managed its own retries independently, with no awareness of what every other request was doing.

### Why we did not catch it sooner

- The SwiftShip status page was green, which sent the investigation down the wrong path for a while. In hindsight, a green status page and a flood of rate limit responses is itself a strong signal that the problem is our traffic.
- The carrier-gateway dashboard showed overall error rate, but did not break errors down by type prominently. The rate limit pattern was visible only once someone drilled into the logs.
- The runbook did not mention rate limiting at all.

### Why it was not caught earlier in design

The rate limit was known, but traffic under normal conditions sat comfortably below it, so it was not treated as a design constraint. Nobody had modelled what happens to outbound volume when a large share of requests fail and retry at the same time.

## What went well

- Warehouse leads reported the problem quickly and clearly, which confirmed customer impact early.
- Once the rate limit pattern was spotted, mitigation was fast and effective.
- Resubmission in batches worked as intended and did not trigger a second storm.
- Communication in the incident channel was regular, and support had enough information to answer customer contacts.

## What went poorly

- Time was lost investigating deploys and credentials before looking closely at the error types.
- Disabling retries was done by hand, under pressure, without a documented procedure.
- The failure mode was entirely self inflicted once the initial slowdown ended.

## Follow ups

| # | Action | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reduce retries in RB-CARRIER-01.** | Lower the maximum retries per carrier request and update the runbook accordingly. The new runbook version should also cover rate limit responses explicitly and describe how to disable and re enable retries safely. |
| 2 | **Write an ADR to cap outbound traffic to SwiftShip.** | No ADR number has been assigned yet at the time of writing. The ADR should decide how carrier-gateway limits its aggregate request rate to SwiftShip so that the total stays under the carrier's limit regardless of how many individual requests are retrying. |
| 3 | Stop retrying rate limit responses immediately. | Rate limit rejections should be handled differently from timeouts and server errors, for example by backing off at the aggregate level. Expected to be covered by the ADR in item 2. |
| 4 | Break down carrier-gateway errors by type on the main dashboard. | Rate limit rejections should be visible at a glance. |
| 5 | Add an alert on rate limit responses from SwiftShip. | A sustained stream of rate limit responses should page directly, with a pointer to the runbook. |

## Lessons

Retries are a load multiplier. A retry policy that is safe for a single request can be dangerous for the fleet, and the safe design has to consider aggregate behaviour, not just per request behaviour. Any integration with an external rate limit should treat that limit as a hard design constraint, even when normal traffic is nowhere near it.
